01 · The approach

Build to learn, not to launch.

Each step is short, and each one is designed to answer a question rather than produce a finished product.

  1. 01 Frame Set out the proposition and name its riskiest assumptions.
  2. 02 Build Produce the smallest working version (MVP) that can test them.
  3. 03 Connect Link it to real (or test) data, in a private sandbox that never touches your live systems.
  4. 04 Share Put it in the hands of customers, staff, partners or funders – it opens from a link, with no app store.
  5. 05 Read See what it reveals about the impact of AI on the market, the customer and the model.
  6. 06 Decide Proceed, change direction or stop, on evidence-based insights, not opinion.

02 · What building reveals

The prototype is your research.

Building it exposes how AI is likely to reshape a business in six ways that a plan on paper tends to miss.

i

Market structure

When AI enters a sector, value moves. Building shows where it moves to:

  • whether accumulated knowledge (data moat) turns out to be the real asset or a potential liability
  • whether dominant platforms and aggregators will grow stronger or get bypassed
  • who ends up owning which parts of the customer journey, and the data that comes with it

ii

Communication

Customers increasingly reach businesses through an AI assistant, chatbot or agent. A prototype tests what that means:

  • how a business is found, described and ranked when a machine sits in between
  • how content must be structured so AI systems can access and read it
  • which channels and tools your customers already use, and which to add

iii

Business models

When AI cuts costs, the question becomes what is still worth paying for:

  • where the margin sits once the build cost falls away
  • which parts are defensible and which a competitor can copy in a week
  • what customers will pay for, as opposed to what they say they want

iv

Regulatory compliance

Building surfaces obligations that a plan can leave vague:

  • which EU AI Act obligations apply, and at what level of risk
  • what GDPR requires for the data the product actually collects
  • which markets and jurisdictions to operate in, given their regulation

v

Operating model

When AI does more of the work, the shape of the business changes:

  • which tasks AI handles reliably and which still need people
  • what resourcing a pilot or launch needs as it grows
  • where human review must stay in the loop for critical oversight

vi

Adoption

What people say in a survey and what they do with a working product differ:

  • whether people come back after the first use, and what needs to improve
  • which features they use, which they ignore and what they suggest
  • whether they would pay, recommend or switch

04 · Work in brief

Three recent propositions.

A consumer app for outdoor and leisure pursuits

Built in-house

The proposition – a mobile app that ranks locations by live conditions for an outdoor activity.

What was built – a working app that installs from a link, drawing on public open data for weather, tides, sea state and geographical mapping, combined with established community-of-practice data.

What it revealed – public open data could replace much of what would once have been bought in. The real competitive asset was the community's knowledge. Building it surfaced safety and liability questions early, which reshaped what the product would recommend. Testing also highlighted quality assurance.

An engine for testing strategic decisions

Private client

The proposition – an orchestrated 10-agent system that tests business plans and strategic options against each other before money is committed.

What was built – a pipeline that runs a plan through a series of analytical passes, each handled by a different, independent AI specialist agent, with every step recorded.

What it revealed – a single AI model's view can be confident and still mislead, and disagreement between independent agents is itself useful evidence. It showed the value of combining different perspectives and analytical tools, and how much an AI-built platform depends on its providers – which models it can access, where data is processed, and on what terms.

A platform for online communities of practice

In development · private client

The proposition – an AI-native platform that lets the owner of an online community of practice build an individualised plan for each member, and tailor service offers to that plan – both the owner's services and those of third-party providers.

What was built – a prototype that draws on a member's goals and context to produce a personal plan, then matches services from the owner and from third-party providers to each dimension in that plan, anticipating needs one member at a time.

What it revealed – when AI can produce content on demand, a community's value moves from what it publishes to how well it knows each member. Personalisation depends on members choosing to share what they want, so consent and trust shape the product as much as the technology does. The offer stops being a catalogue and becomes a sequence fitted to each person. AI has the capacity to anticipate need, creating new value for customers and businesses alike.

The same method applies inside an established business: pick the strategic assumption that matters most, build the smallest thing that tests it, and let the evidence inform the decision.
